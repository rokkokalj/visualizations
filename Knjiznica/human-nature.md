# Human Nature From First Principles

A complete, beginner-friendly, first-principles guide to what humans are, why we are the way we are, and what has actually driven the species across the ~300,000 years we have existed.

The treatment is encyclopedic on purpose. The goal is to build one durable mental model that explains psychology, history, business, politics, religion, technology, and your own behavior using the same underlying primitives.

Every chapter ends with two applied-lens blocks: how the chapter's ideas show up in business and GTM, and how they show up in self-understanding and personal life. The applied blocks are short. The core content is long.

## The thirty-second version

If you only have one minute, here is the entire document compressed.

Humans are biological organisms shaped by roughly 3.5 billion years of evolution. The body and brain you carry now were largely finalized for life as a hunter-gatherer in small bands on the African savanna, somewhere between 300,000 and 70,000 years ago. Evolution does not optimize for happiness or truth. It optimizes for reproductive success in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness.

A cognitive leap roughly 70,000 years ago gave humans language sophisticated enough to talk about things that do not exist. That unlocked cooperation in groups larger than the 150 or so individuals a primate brain can track directly. Money, gods, nations, corporations, brands, ideologies. All of these are shared fictions. They are also the operating system of civilization. No other species has them.

For the next 60,000 years humans roamed in small bands and were relatively egalitarian within them. Then, around 10,000 years ago, agriculture produced food surplus. Surplus produced hierarchy, property, taxes, kings, armies, slavery, and writing. The biology did not change. The environment did. We have been living in an environment our genes were not built for ever since.

Around 2,500 years ago, the Axial Age produced the first formal moral philosophies and universal religions. Around 500 years ago, the Scientific Revolution discovered that observation and math could overrule authority. Around 250 years ago, the Industrial Revolution unleashed energy density that fundamentally changed material life. Now we live in conditions of unprecedented abundance, with brains tuned for scarcity, status competition, and tribal cohesion in small groups.

What drives humans, across every culture and every century, is a stable set of needs: survival, reproduction, status, belonging, meaning, understanding, autonomy, comfort. The cultural surface varies. The drives do not. Status is the single most underestimated driver of adult human behavior.

The clash between our evolved nature and our current environment produces most of what looks like modern dysfunction: obesity, anxiety, depression, loneliness, polarization, addiction, the attention crisis. Civilization is a technology for cooperating at scale despite our nature. It is real, it is fragile, and it is not guaranteed to continue.

That paragraph is true at every scale: an individual life, a company, an empire. Everything else in this document is detail, evidence, and application.

## Source quality note

This is a synthesis document. The macro claims (evolutionary background, broad historical periods, human universals, major drives) are well established across multiple independent fields. The micro claims (specific dates, specific causal stories, contested theories) are flagged where active scholarly debate exists. Where I am uncertain or where the evidence is weaker, the text says so.

Major intellectual lineages drawn on:

| Field | Key contributors I am leaning on |
|---|---|
| Evolutionary biology | Darwin, Hamilton, Trivers, E. O. Wilson, Robert Sapolsky |
| Evolutionary psychology | Cosmides, Tooby, Buss, Pinker |
| Anthropology | Donald Brown (human universals), Joseph Henrich (cultural evolution) |
| Macro-history | Yuval Noah Harari, Jared Diamond, Will and Ariel Durant, William McNeill |

You do not need to have read any of them. The point of this document is that the underlying model is portable across all of them.

## When this document is wrong

A first-principles model is a compression. It will be wrong at the edges. Specifically:

- It will overstate stability of human nature where culture is doing more work than the document credits.
- It will understate cultural variation because the goal is to find what is shared.
- Some claims (the agricultural revolution as a net loss for human welfare, the exact mechanism of the cognitive revolution, the universality of certain behaviors) remain contested. The most credible counter-positions are noted in the relevant chapter.
- Specific dates for prehistoric events have meaningful ranges of uncertainty. When this document says "70,000 years ago" or "10,000 years ago," read it as "tens of thousands of years ago, give or take a few."

The model is good enough to be useful. It is not good enough to be final. Use it as scaffolding, not gospel.

## Applied lens: why bother

Two reasons.

**For business and GTM**, every product is bought by a human, every sale is closed by a human, every team is run by a human. If you do not understand what humans actually want under the surface (status, belonging, identity, relief from anxiety, a story they can tell about themselves), you will keep building products people compliment but do not buy. The strongest marketers, founders, and operators run on a model of human nature whether they articulate it or not. Making the model explicit is leverage.

**For self-understanding**, most of what drives you is unconscious. Your stated reasons for choices are usually constructed afterward. Your status drive is more powerful than you let yourself admit. Your tribal loyalties shape your beliefs more than your beliefs shape your loyalties. Without a clear model of what your evolved nature actually wants, you will keep being surprised by yourself, including in ways that derail your goals.

The rest of this document is the model.
